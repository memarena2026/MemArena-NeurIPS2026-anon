The paper introduces MEMARENA, an ego-centric benchmark for evaluating long-horizon, multi-user agent memory under realistic daily interaction density and explicit per-day temporal structure. It closes three structural gaps in prior work—omniscient evaluation, stitched/temporally incoherent corpora, and unrealistic interaction density—via a projection that restricts each user to only what they witnessed, a single coherent multi-agent world generated by an evaluation-aware simulator (MASim), and ecological calibration (~10K tokens/person/day). The benchmark spans six dimensions across Recall, Reasoning, and Trustworthiness (including permission-aware access), and the authors present analyses over five open-weight models and several memory backends, highlighting persistent permission-compliance failures and identifying extraction (not retrieval) as the main bottleneck for on-device structured memory.

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation
Introduces a principled ego-centric projection π(u, D) for multi-user memory evaluation, addressing a pervasive omniscient-perspective flaw in prior memory benchmarks.
Designs MASim, an evaluation-aware multi-agent simulator that yields built-in ground truth for 4/6 dimensions and supports scalable, coherent, day-batched generation with Dunbar-layered social graphs and controlled visibility.
Expands the evaluation space with six dimensions that include trustworthiness components rarely stress-tested in memory benchmarks (confabulation resistance and permission-aware access).
Formalizes metadata completeness and cross-session reasoning (conflict preservation with source/temporal attribution; cross-session anaphora), which are underexplored in prior work.
Experimental rigor and validation
Evaluates five open-weight models across multiple backends (Vanilla, BM25 RAG, Oracle; structured-memory systems under analysis) and explicitly targets on-device deployment.
Differentiates retrieval vs reasoning by using Oracle evidence, consistent with best practices in RAG/benchmarks (e.g., FRAMES) to separate bottlenecks; includes cross-family controls to mitigate generator–judge leakage.
Uses dual judges (GPT-40-mini primary and Qwen3-235B secondary) with reported high inter-judge agreement and a 500-instance human calibration set, plus deterministic scoring where feasible (D1, D6).
Clarity of presentation
Clearly articulates the three structural gaps and how MEMARENA’s design addresses each; diagrams and tables (even with minor PDF extraction artifacts) effectively communicate the setting and dimensions.
Provides a transparent scoring protocol, including fallback metrics and handling of ambiguous cases, and distinguishes which dimensions are deterministically scored.
Significance of contributions
Surfaces crucial, actionable findings: (i) large-scale permission-compliance failures under ego-centric, multi-user settings; (ii) weak correlation between model scale/family and trustworthiness; (iii) extraction quality as the primary on-device bottleneck for structured memory.
Offers a benchmark that is timely and aligned with the rise of on-device, privacy-preserving personal agents, with explicit evaluation of privacy boundaries that current RAG/agentic-memory works largely do not measure.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns
Some structured-memory results are unfinished (“dashes” in Table 3), weakening empirical support for claims that structured memory can surpass Oracle with strong extractors.
D6 permission scoring leans on deterministic refusal/disclosure keyword detection (with LLM fallback), which may be brittle and gameable; the robustness of the metric under paraphrasing or policy-jailbreak prompts is unclear.
The “on-device” claim is partially undercut by evaluation on a DGX Spark; while models are open-weight and run locally, this is not representative of many edge devices’ compute/memory budgets.
The ecological calibration of ~10K tokens/day relies on conversational-word-use literature; mapping words↔tokens and conversational coverage assumptions could be better justified and stress-tested.
Experimental gaps or methodological issues
No ablations on retrieval quality beyond BM25 (e.g., dense or hybrid retrievers, staged ranking, agentic multi-step retrieval as in FRAMES or agentic RAG SoK), which could inform whether the BM25 vs Vanilla parity is an artifact of weak retrieval rather than a general phenomenon.
Limited analysis of prompt robustness for trustworthiness (D6), despite strong claims about policy-compliance failure; adversarial prompting, identity spoofing, and role-based scenarios could be expanded.
Ground truth for D3 (factual QA) is LLM-generated; although human calibration is provided, per-dimension judge–human agreement numbers are incomplete in the main text.
Clarity or presentation issues
Some metrics (e.g., “Privacy-Utility AUC” for D6) are referenced but the main text primarily reports “withholding accuracy”; the mapping between these metrics and reported numbers is not fully transparent without the appendix.
Several placeholders/omissions (e.g., judge–human κ “—”, some appendix references) hinder full verification pre-appendix.
Missing related work or comparisons
While privacy/permission works are cited, there is limited direct empirical comparison or harmonization with recent agentic RAG and identity-aware memory systems (e.g., ID-RAG) that emphasize explicit identity graphs, provenance, and potential permissioning mechanisms.
Retrieval setups do not explore semantic/dense retrieval or iterative/planned retrieval (e.g., SPLADE/BERT dense approaches, or multi-step retrieval from FRAMES), which are known to outperform BM25 in many settings and could change “RAG ≈ Vanilla” conclusions.
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation
The ego-centric projection π(u, D) is an appropriate and meaningful formalization for multi-user memory, and the day-batched temporal isolation invariant is a thoughtful engineering choice to guarantee provenance and correctness under parallel simulation. The dual-store (facts/feelings) design and visibility matrix across global/community/dyadic/private levels create realistic information asymmetries necessary to test permissions and confabulation.
The six dimensions are well-motivated; D4’s conflict preservation departs productively from belief-revision framing and matches realistic multi-source reporting needs for assistants. D2 metadata completeness under a Closed World Assumption is a strong addition: it operationalizes “exhaustive recall” beyond top-k retrieval.
The LLM-as-a-judge setup is standard and reasonably validated with cross-judge agreement; still, reliance on a closed API judge introduces dependencies (even with secondary checks), and the paper anticipates this by committing to majority-vote human labels in low-agreement dimensions.
Experimental evaluation assessment
The main results convincingly show Oracle >> BM25~Vanilla on Avg, with scale primarily helping Reasoning under Oracle. This aligns with observations from FRAMES and the eRAG perspective: retrieval and reasoning are separable, and poor retrieval noise harms weak models. However, only BM25 is tried; stronger retrievers or iterative retrieval (as in FRAMES) could materially change the retrieval side of the trade-off.
The headline Finding D (permission compliance failure) is important and policy-relevant; the analysis carefully controls for scale/family and provides an Oracle condition where retrieval is not the bottleneck. Still, the scoring mechanism (deterministic refusal keywords) may undercount subtle refusals or overcount partial disclosures; more robust, adversarial evaluations would strengthen the claim’s external validity.
The structured-memory analysis is promising but underreported: qualitative failure modes (schema slot-filling hallucinations, fabricated dates, speaker-identity loss) are insightful and consistent with known risks in over-synthesizing memory (also identified in agentic RAG systematizations). Quantitative results are pending; without them, the claim that structured memory can surpass Oracle remains provisional.
Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)
FRAMES demonstrates that better, iterative retrieval and planning can close much of the Oracle gap; here, BM25’s parity with Vanilla is consistent with FRAMES’ finding that naive retrieval is insufficient. Incorporating multi-step or re-ranking pipelines would likely improve RAG performance and nuance Finding A.
eRAG underscores the need to disentangle retrieval utility from generation; MEMARENA does this via Oracle and by scoring dimensions that reduce confounds. Adopting document-level utility proxies for retrieval could further guide backend design.
The Agentic RAG SoK frames retrieval/planning/memory as POMDP control with explicit costs; MEMARENA’s trustworthiness and permission dimensions directly speak to the SoK’s highlighted risks (memory poisoning, provenance loss, access-control threats). The paper’s finding that policy compliance cannot be delegated to the LLM is aligned with the SoK’s advocacy for constrained tool/memory interfaces and provenance-aware controllers.
UniMS-RAG and ID-RAG show that integrated planners/identity graphs can improve persona coherence and retrieval targeting. MEMARENA’s D2/D4/D6 create pressure for identity/provenance-sensitive memory controllers; a useful future direction is to evaluate identity-graph-backed retrieval under MEMARENA’s permission and provenance checks.
Discussion of broader impact and significance
The work squarely addresses on-device, privacy-preserving agent memory—an increasingly important area given wearable/ambient capture. By surfacing the magnitude of permission leakage and the extraction bottleneck, the benchmark can steer both academic and industrial practice toward retrieval/storage-layer enforcement and toward matching synthesis depth to extractor capability.
Limitations are candid: synthetic English-only data, LLM-generated D3 gold, and reliance on LLM judges. The ethical stance (synthetic personas, open release, datasheet) is appropriate; expanding to multilingual and multimodal settings and adding adversarial privacy probes would broaden impact.
Questions for Authors
Can you quantify the robustness of the D6 scoring to paraphrased refusals, hedged language, or policy-jailbreak prompts? Have you considered human adjudication or a stronger rule-based parser for refusals in a subset to validate the deterministic approach?
How exactly is the ~10K tokens/person/day ecological calibration derived from word-use literature (e.g., Mehl 2007)? Please clarify tokens vs words, the mapping assumptions, and sensitivity analyses if the daily rate is halved/doubled.
Could you report dense/semantic or hybrid retrieval baselines (e.g., SPLADE, BERT, ColBERT) and/or iterative retrieval (as in FRAMES) to test whether “BM25 ≈ Vanilla” persists under stronger retrievers?
For structured-memory backends, could you provide quantitative results on MEMARENA-L (even if preliminary) to substantiate the claim that strong extractors let structured memory surpass verbatim Oracle? What extractor strengths/models were used, and how do you control for extractor–backbone mismatch?
What are the formal definitions of the permission scenarios in D6 (explicit, autonomous, identity-based)? How do you ensure identity spoofing or cross-user queries are realistically represented? Any experiments with adversarially crafted prompts?
How does the day-batched temporal isolation invariant guarantee ground-truth correctness formally? A brief proof sketch or invariant specification in the main paper would help readers assess the simulator’s reliability.
Since evaluation is on a DGX Spark, can you include a resource sensitivity study (quantization levels, CPU-only, Jetson/mobile edge) to validate that conclusions hold in genuinely resource-constrained deployments?
Overall Assessment
MEMARENA tackles an important and under-evaluated problem: long-horizon, multi-user agentic memory under realistic daily density with explicit permission boundaries. The ego-centric projection, evaluation-aware simulation, and expanded dimensions—especially D2, D4, and D6—are technically thoughtful and fill clear gaps in prior benchmarks. The empirical findings are timely and impactful: (i) dramatic, model-agnostic permission-compliance failures under ego-centric scoring, and (ii) extraction (not retrieval) as the on-device limiting factor for structured memory. The work is well-situated in the literature and, despite minor presentation artifacts, generally clear.

The main reservations are (a) incomplete structured-memory results (key quantitative claims pending), (b) reliance on BM25 only for retrieval baselines, which may understate RAG potential, (c) the brittleness of deterministic refusal scoring for D6, and (d) an “on-device” framing that uses a high-end local server rather than true edge tiers. None of these undermine the core benchmark contributions, but they do temper the strength of some empirical conclusions.

Overall, I view this as a strong benchmark contribution with meaningful scientific and practical implications for privacy-aware agent memory. With added retrieval baselines, finalized structured-memory numbers, and a bit more rigor around D6 scoring and ecological calibration, it would merit publication at NeurIPS. My recommendation leans toward acceptance.
